# Why I write so little

I haven't been feeling very well for a few days now, ever since I got back from the Netherlands four days ago. That is not due to missing home (the Netherlands) that much, or being sick of Linz upon return; at least, not as far as I know. I also cannot really say, or wouldn't want to say, I'm really sick — I wouldn't want to say this qualifies as actually being sick, and there is more needed to be allowed to say "I'm sick" — I'm just feeling beaten up, tired, like halfway on the way to sickness. Then again, I wouldn't know when some feeling actually qualifies as "being sick". I also asked a (admittedly simple but still useful) Large Language Model what a single word would be for "feeling under the weather", and it's answer was pretty clear: "sick".

Just the difficulty with finding the right words made me consider how difficult it is to describe physical sensations or feelings, using this public tool called "language". And with feelings I don't mean it in the way you always hear people say "talk about your feelings"; in the case of _physical_ feelings it is usually assumed that it would be easy to talk about, and that only _emotional_ feelings are what people do not talk about. Putting aside any qualms with the "physical" and "emotional" distinction (shouldn't we be saying it's all physical?), I wanted to write down some thoughts on using language for our feelings, and what it implies. 

## The mind-body distinction is a misconception

In philosophy we are traditionally taught, since Descartes, to think about the "mind-body" distinction, and this is still the distinction so pervasive in so many discussions (philosophy of mind, discussions about AI, and the distinction of talking about "your feelings" in the sense mentioned above). I think the distinction that is much more interesting is the distinction between sensations and social, feelings and language, what I would call "the private", and language and communication, "the public". Or, if you will: physical and thought.

I can't get much more further in describing my "sickishness" than being a very vague feeling of tiredness, "my body doesn't want to", "I'm feeling weak". The furthest, or the most useful, I can get to is to provide some kind of proof: I normally would be super active, but now do not feel like doing more than a walk. It isn't a cold though — I just had a cold a few weeks ago, so I guess I should know and be qualified to say that. 

Another way of looking at it, is to see if we have some descriptions for these feelings in language and if they "fit". I have, like mentioned earlier, qualms with "sick". I guess "feeling under the weather" would fit (the weather having changed quite a bit lately and winter is coming), but is that really enough? If I would be signed up for a bike race, I wouldn't cancel because of a slight unease, but would go for a convincing "I'm sick". I guess the usage and context dictates the appropriate phrasing more than my feelings here. And multiple chatbots, which are trained on lots of different language-use, do insist that the word corresponding to "under the weather" is, indeed, "sick".

## Talking about our feelings

The mind-body dilemma is of course the question of how our "thoughts" can be related to the "physical" world, and it has been discussed many times already to be resting on a misconception in many ways. For one, you would have to start with the conception of us having a separate "mind", distinct from our body, anyway, to draw this distinction. Yet it is still pretty pervasive in many conversations (and in what I like to refer to, borrowing from southpark, as "new-age hippie nonsense"). What drew me to think again about this dichotomy, is that it assumes that our "mental life" is unproblematically linked to language, and our thoughts are immediately accessible to us.

In my view, language is public first, and our thoughts are formed and also related in a complex way to this public practice or tool. We learn to use it for specific ways (be that scientific, business, or "normal" usage) and apply to it to the sensations we have; interestingly, it also shapes our sensations in fundamental ways, and the language we use shapes our perceptions. Or our perceptions of our perceptions. This matters because I have frequently heard that people should talk more about their feelings — by this presumably meaning emotions — but this is only possible if we have the language to do so. And this is something one learns how to do; it is a quite specific language-use that, perhaps, younger people have learned better than older people. Maybe "boomers" have never used, or never learned to use, language for their feelings, and don't know how to express them.

Another important reason why I wanted to write this down, is that I have frequently heard it said, recently, that you are either sick or not sick. Yet I don't know what the meaning of "sick" is here precisely, and it usually comes down to "being able to work". Circular definitions not being my favorite, I would say I am frequently able to work from home, but too "sick" to go to the office. If the definition of "sick" would be given by a medical practitioner, I had better sharpen up by skills of talking about my feelings.

## Why I write so little

Back to the title. Also interesting about today is that I always find myself cramped, and do not know where to do things like write in my definitely-not-daily diary. At least, that is what I tell myself just before I also get caught scrolling videos on youtube. Yet today I was somehow already unsure what to do at 9 o'clock, and found myself with "too much time". And I am just all too frequently unsure whether what I am thinking of writing, is something others would want to read. I guess only when I'm under the weather (let's just call it that), do I sit down to start writing thoughts down — a bit sad, isn't it?
